# The Clear Lake Storm-Surge Markers: Historical and Technical Dossier for AR Reconstruction

## Executive summary

The Clear Lake markers were real, unusually dramatic **predictive storm-surge markers** erected after Hurricane Ike as part of a joint federal/state public-awareness effort. Contemporary reporting describes a **$56,000 federal-and-state project** intended to place large storm-surge markers in public locations along the upper Texas coast; later reporting based on a Texas Department of Public Safety spokesperson says the state and FEMA built **hundreds** of markers before the program was discontinued. Six markers were planned for the Houston portion of Clear Lake, but the strongest contemporary evidence identifies only **two confirmed installations**: one on **Clear Lake City Boulevard near Space Center Boulevard**, and one on **Bay Area Boulevard near Clear Lake High School**. citeturn21search0turn2search0

The Houston installations were already controversial by an October 2010 town hall. Mayor Annise Parker halted installation of the remaining Clear Lake markers while the city reassessed their placement and possible “misinterpretation,” and City of Houston crews removed the two specifically identified markers around **mid-January 2011**. Residents and real-estate interests objected both to the implied flood risk and its potential effect on home values. Contemporary sources do **not** support the stronger story that FEMA was forced by Realtors to order the markers removed; the documented removal was done by Houston, after local complaints and lobbying of Councilmember Mike Sullivan. citeturn26search0turn25search5

The scientific controversy was more nuanced than “the marker was wrong.” The markers were labeled **Category 4** and **Category 5**, but just as they were being deployed, the National Hurricane Center was abandoning the practice of associating particular surge ranges with Saffir-Simpson categories. NHC explained in 2009 that category-specific surge ranges were “frequently incorrect” because surge also depends on storm size, track and landfall location; the modern Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale therefore measures wind only. Councilmember Sullivan’s contemporaneous explanation that the marker language was inappropriate for the scales to be used “going forward” fits this national change extremely well, although I found no project document explicitly saying that the NHC change caused Houston’s decision. citeturn27search11turn27search14turn25search5

Crucially, **that does not mean 20–25 feet of water in the Clear Lake/Galveston Bay system was scientifically implausible**. The contemporary *Bay Area Citizen* report says a state official characterized the marker's 25-foot surge as a worst-case, approximately 500-year event. Later independent FEMA/USACE and Rice/SSPEED modeling examined extreme tracks capable of producing roughly **25 feet or more in parts of Clear Lake and still larger levels farther up the bay/Ship Channel system**. Those later models cannot be assumed to be the data source for the 2010 marker, but they undermine the idea that the pole was simply an absurd exaggeration. citeturn26search0turn2search0turn2search3

## Program origins, funding, and chronology

Hurricane Ike made landfall on the upper Texas coast on September 13, 2008. Ike demonstrated particularly starkly why wind category is a poor proxy for surge: it was a Category 2 hurricane at Texas landfall yet generated much more serious coastal inundation than “Category 2” sounded like to many members of the public. NHC's contemporaneous advisory warned of coastal surge flooding potentially reaching **20 feet**, and in bays and rivers potentially **25 feet**, in the most exposed locations; subsequent NHC summaries describe roughly 15–20 feet above normal in portions of the Galveston Bay region. citeturn15search18turn15search9

The marker program followed Ike as a public-awareness measure. The February 1, 2011 *Bay Area Citizen/Houston Chronicle* report is the strongest contemporary account I found. It identifies the blue-and-green markers as part of a **$56,000 “federal and state project” intended to raise coastal residents’ awareness of storm surge**. A 2016 ProPublica/Texas Tribune investigation, based in part on interviews with Texas officials, specifies FEMA and the state as the partners and describes the effort as building **hundreds of storm-surge markers for public locations on the upper Texas coast**. Texas DPS spokesperson Tom Vinger told the reporters the program was eventually “discontinued due to lack of community interest.” citeturn21search0turn2search0

That is the limit of what I can substantiate about the funding mechanism. I searched indexed FEMA, TDEM/Texas DPS, post-Ike disaster, hazard-mitigation and Houston sources but did **not** find an award record identifying a grant number or an official project title. FEMA disaster declaration DR-1791-TX covered Hurricane Ike, but the mere existence of DR-1791 does not establish that this $56,000 education project was funded through a particular DR-1791 Public Assistance or Hazard Mitigation Grant Program award. It would be an overreach to call it an HMGP project without the missing grant file. FEMA's current OpenFEMA HMA system does document project-level mitigation grants in general, while TDEM's Hazard Mitigation Section administers planning and mitigation grants in Texas, but neither public search produced the marker project itself. citeturn23search4turn23search3

Nor can the available $56,000 figure be converted responsibly into a cost per marker. “Hundreds” is not an exact quantity, the sources do not establish whether $56,000 included fabrication, installation, design, shipping, administration or all of those costs, and it is unclear how many fabricated markers were actually installed before local participation fell apart. citeturn2search0turn21search0

## Surge science, model provenance, and vertical-datum problem

### What we know about the marker's science

The contemporary source makes one unusually important technical statement: the Category 4/5 marker was designed to show water associated with a **25-foot storm surge**, and an unnamed state official characterized that as a **worst-case scenario associated with a 500-year event**. That is currently the closest thing to a description of the design basis. citeturn26search0

What the source does **not** tell us is equally important. It does not name SLOSH, HAZUS, a FEMA Flood Insurance Study, a hurricane evacuation study, an ADCIRC simulation, a particular synthetic storm, a tide condition, or a vertical datum. I therefore found **no basis for saying that the pole was definitely a SLOSH marker**.

SLOSH is nevertheless a plausible candidate for the planning data behind the concept. NHC's long-standing MEOW and MOM framework is explicitly designed for hurricane evacuation planning. A MEOW combines multiple hypothetical SLOSH tracks while holding category, forward speed, trajectory and initial tide assumptions constant; a MOM then takes maxima across MEOWs for a given hurricane category. In other words, **Category 4 and Category 5 worst-case planning surfaces are exactly the kind of product SLOSH can generate**. But that conceptual match is an inference, not documentary proof of this marker's source. citeturn27search4turn27search1

NHC is emphatic about the interpretation: a MEOW is a worst-case envelope assembled from multiple hypothetical tracks, and **no single hurricane will produce the entire regional flooding pattern depicted by a MEOW**. Similarly, a MOM is a category-specific near-worst-case planning envelope rather than a forecast of one storm. citeturn27search7turn27search1

That distinction may explain part of the Clear Lake reaction. A public marker has no map legend explaining ensemble maxima, track uncertainty, tide assumptions or return periods. It converts a probabilistic/planning abstraction into a giant physical object that looks like a simple promise: “A Category 5 puts the water here.” Contemporary officials were simultaneously discovering that this category-to-surge shorthand was not scientifically sound. That interpretation is consistent with the sources but remains an inference. citeturn26search0turn27search11

### Why the Saffir-Simpson wording became obsolete

NHC's May 2009 notice is unusually direct. It proposed deleting storm-surge ranges and flooding references from all five categories because those surge ranges were often inconsistent with storms' wind categories and could mislead preparedness decisions; NHC specifically cited factors such as **storm size and landfall location**. The revised system became the **Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale**, which today is explicitly based only on sustained wind speed. citeturn27search11turn27search14

That puts Sullivan's 2010–2011 statements in context. At the October town hall he said the sign language was “not appropriate any more,” and in the ABC13 interview he said the problem was the wording relative to the hurricane scales officials would use going forward—not simply whether residents liked the signs or whether the risk itself was right or wrong. citeturn26search0turn25search5

So the most defensible hackathon wording is:

> **The marker's extreme-surge hazard was not disproved. Its Category 4/Category 5 communication scheme became scientifically problematic because hurricane wind category does not uniquely determine storm surge.** citeturn27search11turn27search14

### Was 25 feet unrealistic?

Not inherently. ProPublica and the Texas Tribune's later Houston investigation examined two different extreme-surge models. Rice's SSPEED Center/University of Texas **“Mighty Ike” or P7+15** scenario modified an Ike-like storm and produced water exceeding 25 feet above sea level in portions of the Clear Lake/Galveston Bay system; a separate FEMA/USACE synthetic-storm analysis produced even higher levels in some locations. The article describes the scenarios as rare but physically meaningful planning cases rather than Category 5 hurricanes. citeturn2search0turn2search3turn2search4

Those later calculations **must not be retroactively substituted for the marker's missing source model**. Their value is narrower: they show that the basic idea “Clear Lake can face an extreme 20–30-foot-class surge/water-level hazard under an unfavorable major hurricane” did not disappear when the marker was taken down. citeturn2search0turn2search3

Blackburn makes essentially that argument retrospectively. His 2023 Baker paper says the marker was not removed because the underlying danger had been shown to be wrong; he characterizes the warning as “right on target.” That is Blackburn's expert retrospective judgment, not evidence of the original TDEM engineering basis, and it should be presented as such. citeturn25search0

### The critical AR question: what does “25 feet” mean vertically?

This is the most important unresolved technical issue for your application.

There are at least three quantities that people casually call “a 25-foot surge”:

**Storm-surge height relative to a reference water level.** This describes the abnormal rise of coastal water generated by the storm.

**Absolute water-surface elevation**, for example 25 ft NAVD88 or another datum.

**Inundation depth above local ground**, which might be only several feet at a location whose terrain elevation is already well above the vertical datum.

The 2011 articles do not tell us which quantity was physically encoded on the Clear Lake marker. citeturn26search0turn25search5

That ambiguity is not academic. USGS explicitly notes that storm-surge observations from a large coastal region have to be brought to a **common national vertical datum**; a local datum is not adequate for combining measurements, and post-hurricane surveying uses GNSS/leveling to establish consistent water elevations. citeturn24search6

Modern NHC products make the distinction much clearer. Operational Potential Storm Surge Flooding products present inundation **above ground level**, while probabilistic guidance has also been provided relative to **NAVD88**. NHC uses high-resolution elevation data to turn water-level modeling into maps of flooding over normally dry ground. citeturn27search5turn27search17turn27search20

That leads to a practical AR rule:

**Do not use “25 ft” from the 2011 newspaper as an absolute AR `z` coordinate.**

For a historically faithful **visual replica of the pole**, a provisional ~25-ft object is defensible if you explicitly label its dimensions approximate. For a scientifically meaningful **water surface**, use either:

`water-surface elevation in a known datum − terrain elevation in the same datum = depth above ground`

or use an authoritative dataset that already gives **depth above ground**. USGS/NHC guidance demonstrates why mixing vertical reference systems without this transformation can produce misleading results. citeturn24search6turn27search20

Modern NHC SLOSH-based national risk maps are also useful as context but not as parcel-level truth: NHC creates category-specific MOM inundation products, using high-tide scenarios and one-foot depth bins, while explicitly warning that the data are for community-level planning/awareness rather than parcel-level determinations. citeturn27search8

## Controversy, removal, and the Blackburn connection

The controversy had **two overlapping strands** that are sometimes collapsed into one story.

The first was economic and psychological. Contemporary reporting says some residents and real-estate agents feared the markers could depress property values. ABC13 interviewed resident Susan Maki, who said she simply did not believe water would get that high, and reported that local real estate professionals were fielding worried calls from prospective or current residents at a time of economic anxiety associated with NASA layoffs. citeturn25search5turn26search0

Todd Hinz, identified as a Bay Oaks resident, lobbied Houston Councilmember Mike Sullivan for months. Hinz's objection was partly comparative: Ike had not produced anything resembling the marker's displayed depth in Clear Lake, whereas places such as Kemah and Seabrook that had experienced dramatic Ike impacts did not have such signs. ABC13 quotes him arguing that outsiders might see the markers and conclude that something was wrong with Clear Lake. citeturn25search5

The second strand was legitimate risk-communication science. Sullivan's explanation was not “the rich homeowners made me do it.” He told ABC13 that the issue was not simply whether the signs were right or wrong or liked or disliked, but that their **language was inappropriate for the scales officials would use for future hurricanes**. That explanation is highly consistent with NHC's contemporaneous removal of surge ranges from the Saffir-Simpson scale. citeturn25search5turn27search11

Mayor Annise Parker had already halted the rest of the six planned Houston/Clear Lake installations in 2010. Her letter, as quoted contemporaneously, said the city was **“reassessing the markers and their locations due to possible misinterpretation.”** citeturn26search0

Around mid-January 2011, **City of Houston workers** removed the two markers identified by the newspaper. ### What is most useful for the AR model right now

For tomorrow's hackathon, there is enough evidence to build two deliberately different versions.

The first can be a **historical reconstruction**, clearly labeled approximate. Use the Blackburn silhouette, blue lower body, green upper body, prominent vertical Category 4/5 lettering, and a provisional overall scale in the **20–25-foot range**. Do not invent detailed tick marks, manufacturer logos or top-panel wording. The correct claim is “reconstruction from surviving photograph and contemporary descriptions,” not “digitally exact replica.” citeturn25search0turn26search0turn12search2

The second should be the actual **AR flood-information concept**, which fixes the old marker's principal communication weakness. Rather than saying merely “Category 5,” the AR overlay can identify the scenario:

> **Historical Clear Lake FEMA/Texas marker — reconstructed**  
> Extreme surge planning scenario, circa 2010  
> Original model/datum still under archival investigation

and for modern data:

> **Modeled inundation: 12 ft above ground**  
> Source: NHC SLOSH/P-Surge  
> Scenario/probability: displayed explicitly  
> Terrain reference: current geospatial ground surface

That design follows modern NHC practice of separating wind category from storm-surge risk and presenting operational inundation in terms of **water above ground**, while preserving the extraordinary communicative insight of the old physical pole: let a person look up and comprehend where the water actually is. citeturn27search14turn27search20turn27search21

For the hackathon narrative, this is a source-supported formulation:

> **After Hurricane Ike, FEMA and Texas funded a storm-surge-awareness project that placed unusually tall Category 4/5 surge markers in Clear Lake. Six were planned in Houston; contemporary reporting identifies at least two installations. Residents and real-estate interests objected to the markers' effect on perceptions of the community, while officials also recognized that category-based surge wording no longer reflected emerging National Hurricane Center guidance. Houston halted further installation and removed the two documented signs in early 2011. Rice University flood expert Jim Blackburn continued using one of the markers as an example of the difficulty of communicating Houston's flood risk and preserved a photograph of it. Our AR project takes the original idea—making invisible risk visible at human scale—and puts the marker back virtually, with modern geospatial data and explicit scenario, probability and vertical-reference information.** citeturn26search0turn25search5turn27search11turn25search7turn25search0

That last sentence is where your project's history and technology meet. The most compelling version is not “FEMA put up a scary pole and Realtors made them remove it.” The evidence supports a better story: **officials tried to make an extreme but legitimate hazard physically comprehensible; the visualization was politically explosive and scientifically oversimplified; it disappeared; and AR now makes it possible to restore the visualization while improving the science behind what it communicates.**
